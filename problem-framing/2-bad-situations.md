# 2. Bad Situations

*Author: Fidan Huseynova*

The scenarios below follow two people:
- **Keti**, an MIT student who wants to make an impact by joining an early-stage startup.
- **Bumba**, a BU student founder looking for a technical co-founder.

## Bad situation 1: Choice overload

Keti decides she would rather join a startup than spend another summer at a big tech company. She doesn't know any founders personally, so she tries a web search, asks a few friends, and then turns to her university's resources. MIT Orbit's "Launchpad of ideas and startups" lists roughly **500 teams**, but most entries show only a name, a tag and maybe one line of description. Nothing on the list tells her whether a team is looking for someone like her, how much time they expect, or whether the founders care about the same problems she does.

To find a good match, Keti would have to click into listings one by one, read each description, and then try to work out who the people behind it are. After a dozen or so she still hasn't found a clear fit, and the list doesn't seem to get any shorter. She gets overwhelmed and either reaches out to a team that is "good enough" or gives up and accepts the big-tech offer.

**Why this is a bad situation:** a motivated builder who wants to join a startup ends up not joining one, or joins a poor fit that is likely to fall apart. Founders lose a candidate they never knew existed. The cost comes from the effort and lack of signal in the search, not from any missing feature.

## Bad situation 2: The right people are one T stop away

Startup networking events, clubs and listing boards are almost always scoped to **one organization**: one university, one club or one accelerator cohort. Keti goes to MIT pitch nights and reads MIT listings, so she only meets MIT founders.

Meanwhile, Bumba at BU is building exactly the kind of data tool Keti is passionate about and needs an engineer with her skills. They are one T stop apart, but no event, board or club puts them in the same room, so they never meet. Bumba keeps searching inside BU's smaller pool, and Keti keeps scrolling MIT's.

**Why this is a bad situation:** the best match for both people exists nearby, yet the way people search keeps them in separate silos. Both settle for a worse match or no match, even though the Boston area has one of the densest student startup communities anywhere.

## Bad situation 3: Ideas get buried

Founders face the mirror image of Keti's problem. Bumba's idea is one of hundreds competing for attention, and in a job market as competitive as today's, many of the people who do reach out want a resume line, not a long-term commitment to the vision. Bumba spends weeks on coffee chats with candidates whose interest fades once a paid internship comes through. Meanwhile, the people who would genuinely care about the problem never see the pitch, because it sits on page 12 of a listing board.

**Why this is a bad situation:** promising ideas stall, or move forward with mismatched teams, because founders can't reach or identify the builders who share their passion. Early-stage teams often fail over team problems, and here the team starts out on weak footing before any product work has begun.
